COP31 runs into a third day of overtime without a deal on fossil fuels

Nearly 200 countries are still negotiating in Antalya this Sunday, two days after the official close. The latest draft keeps the financial duty of rich countries but strips out the figures, and any mention of fossil fuels is still missing. The total CO2 emissions map helps show the real weight of each bloc.

The climate summit was due to close on Friday 20 November at 18:00. On Sunday morning it was still open. The Turkish-Australian presidency put forward a new text and called the plenary for 09:00 local time (06:00 GMT), according to EFE. The document restates the financial duty of rich countries, but without figures, and stays silent on oil, gas and coal.

From the first draft to the third

The opening text, circulated on Monday 16, ran to 144 paragraphs and contained two explicit references to moving away from fossil fuels. Thursday's revision came in at 80 paragraphs with no mention at all, after objections from Saudi Arabia and other oil and gas producers, according to Reuters. The proposed goal of $120bn a year for adaptation by 2035 also disappeared.

The opposing camp is sizeable. The European Union publicly rejected both early drafts, together with the African bloc and several Latin American countries. India is among the large emitters refusing to name hydrocarbons, according to EFE. Panama has threatened a formal objection and, because COP decisions are taken by consensus, a single country can block the text. The United States is absent for a second year running.

Who emits and who negotiates

The map ranks countries by fossil CO2 emissions in 2024, the Global Carbon Project series published by Our World in Data. It excludes land use and non-fossil CO2, and a minority of small territories has no figure. Shares are calculated against the project’s global total, not against the sum of the countries drawn.

The first finding is concentration. China emits 12,289 million tonnes, 31.8% of the total. The United States, 4,904 (12.7%), and India, 3,193 (8.3%). Together, three countries account for 52.8% of the global total. The top ten account for 68.9%. Any deal that hopes to move the global needle has to run through those capitals, even if the Antalya text does not name them.

The second finding is that the geography of the blockage does not match the ranking. Saudi Arabia emits 692 million tonnes, 1.8% of the total, well below Japan (962) or Indonesia (812), and has been one of the brakes on naming fossil fuels. Its footprint per person, 20.4 tonnes, is nine times India's (2.2) and almost triple Germany’s (6.8). The map does not say who blocks what: it only puts a number on each position.

The three biggest emitters on the map account for 52.8% of global fossil CO2. The draft being negotiated in Antalya this Sunday does not mention fossil fuels in a single paragraph.

What is still to be settled

Three knots remain. The first is the language on fossil fuels: whether a softened formula returns that lets Panama and the group of twelve countries, France and Spain among them, drop their objection without losing the producers. The second is money: the draft speaks of tripling adaptation finance but gives no amount and no method, while the UN estimates developing countries need about $305bn a year by 2035. Adaptation finance itself fell from $81.5bn in 2022 to $64.1bn in 2024, according to the UN Climate Action Yearbook. The third is carbon capture, which the EU will accept mentioning but not with targets attached.

The overtime carries a political cost. A close without a mention of fossil fuels would leave the summit below what was agreed in Dubai in 2023, when COP28 first called for a transition away from fossil fuels. And whatever text emerges from Antalya will have to live with the numbers on the map: the countries that emit most are also the ones that have to move most for the arithmetic to change.
