Oil, stocks and rates: inflation is not one number

Brent fell 5.85% and Wall Street had its best session since April. The energy relief arrives just before US October CPI, but the annual inflation map is a reminder that the same barrel lands in 187 economies with very different price regimes.

Newsroom · 10 November 2026 · 5 min read

Monday 9 November had two unlikely leads: oil and technology. The S&P 500 rose 2.35% to 7,549.94, the Nasdaq 2.74% to 25,253.14 and the Dow Jones 2.15% to 52,389.75. All 11 sectors closed higher. The trigger was crude: Brent fell 5.85% to $92.57 and WTI 4.38% to $86.85 after Donald Trump said he saw little likelihood of ordering fresh strikes on Iran. Nvidia was the exception: it fell 4.2% on the investigation into chief executive Jensen Huang.

What moved the market

The thread linking stocks and rates is oil. A cheaper barrel lowers the energy bill and some of the pressure on prices. Reuters notes that October's jobs report —165,000 positions created— gave the Federal Reserve room to keep raising rates: the market still puts the probability of a 25-basis-point hike in December above 80%, according to CME FedWatch.

Why oil matters so much for CPI

Energy is not the largest component of the basket, but it is one of the most visible and fastest to pass through. Petrol, diesel, heating and transport absorb the oil price within weeks. That is why a 5.85% fall in Brent is read through an inflation lens, even though the effect depends on taxes, subsidies, exchange rates and car use.

The calendar puts that to the test this week. The Labor Department publishes October CPI on Tuesday and producer prices on Friday. Economists polled by Reuters expect a monthly rise of 0.3% in the headline index and 0.2% in the core measure. If the reading surprises to the upside, the oil relief may last only a session.

The map: 187 inflations, not one

The choropleth map brings together the World Bank's latest annual figure for 187 countries and territories. The median is 3.2%, but the distribution has a long tail: 23 economies record inflation at or above 10% and 12 are above 20%. The single global number discussed in Frankfurt or Washington does not represent most of the map.

Comparing large economies makes the point. The United States stands at 2.95% (2024), Germany at 2.17% and Spain at 2.67% (2025 data). Türkiye, at 34.88% in 2025, and Argentina, at 219.88% in 2024, operate in another league. The same shock does not produce the same response at a central bank chasing 2% and in an economy with double-digit inflation.

How this was calculated. All 205 polygons in the GeoJSON were read; 187 have a numeric value. The median is 3.2%, and the counts (23 above 10%, 12 above 20%) use those 187. The years do not match: 167 countries have 2025 data, seven have 2024 and the rest fall between 2011 and 2022. The map compares price regimes; it does not measure October CPI or the oil effect.

The Fed does not respond to the crude price itself but to the inflation left after energy. If oil falls and the core measure does not budge, a rate hike stays on the table. The map does not anticipate Tuesday's reading; it only shows how similar the economies sharing the same barrel really are.

What to watch this week

US CPI on Tuesday and producer prices on Friday can move rates and stocks. Nvidia reports on 17 November and several AI-linked technology firms publish results this week. For crude, the key remains Iran: any incident in Hormuz sends the barrel back towards the top of the table.

The most useful reading of Monday is not that stocks rebounded, but that they did so with oil falling and rates still pointing higher. That combination only works while inflation cooperates.
