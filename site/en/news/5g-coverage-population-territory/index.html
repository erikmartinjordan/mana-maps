5G reaches 99.27% of Spaniards, not 99.27% of Spain

The order that relieves Telefónica of its obligation to provide universal broadband and fixed voice from 2027 cites, among its arguments, 5G coverage of “99.27% of the national territory”. The ministry publishes the same figure as a share of the population, and field measurements put geographic coverage at about half. The registry of 69,785 masts helps explain why.

Newsroom · 28 November 2026 · 6 min read

The BOE published Order TDF/1266/2026 on 25 November. It ends, from 1 January 2027, Telefónica’s obligation to provide broadband and fixed voice as a universal service. The decision rests on a CNMC report of 14 October, eleven submissions and a minimum of 100 Mbps down and 5 Mbps up. The social tariff survives only for existing beneficiaries.

The argument it keeps repeating is coverage. The order states that “5G mobile network coverage reached 99.27% of the national territory”, and 98.10% for standalone 5G. The same 99.27% appears in the ministry’s 2025 broadband coverage report, but there it refers to people: 99.27% of the population, 96.13% of the rural population and 98.1% for 5G SA.

A share of residents is not a share of land. The question is how much of Spain 5G covers, and there are three ways to answer it: official documents, field measurements and the mast registry.

Method and data

The analysis draws on Order TDF/1266/2026 and the CNMC report it cites; the ministry’s 2025 broadband coverage report (6 May) and nPerf’s September 2026 barometer, with measurements from 1 September 2025 to 31 August 2026; and the Infoantenas (VCTEL) registry of 9 November, which the map groups into 220 points. The only calculation of our own is the sum of installations per operator. Adslzone flagged the discrepancy on 25 November.

The same number, two denominators

The ministry’s release is headlined “5G now reaches 99% of Spain’s population and 96% in rural areas”. The report sets out 99.27% of the population, 96.13% in rural areas and 98.1% for 5G SA. The order, by contrast, says “territory”, as does the CNMC report it reproduces. Neither publishes a measurement of surface area. The 99.27% does not say that almost all of Spain has 5G: it says that almost all residents live where there is 5G.

What field measurements show

nPerf measures real users, not theoretical maps. In September 2026, the best measured geographic 5G coverage in Spain was 50.31% (MasOrange/Yoigo) and Vodafone’s was 50.29%; their population coverage was 72.71% and 70.86%. The gap is about 22 points. In 4G the best operator covers 91.57% of the territory its users travel through and 96.61% of the population. The 50.31% is not a census: nPerf divides the country into parcels of 400 to 600 metres and only counts those its Android users visit: a sample, not a census.

The network follows the people

The Infoantenas registry shows where the installations are: 69,785 masts. Vodafone accounts for 25,192 (36.1%), Movistar for 23,058 (33.0%) and Orange for 19,021 (27.3%); three networks make up 96.4%. Madrid holds 7,581 (10.9%), Barcelona 5,913 (8.5%) and Valencia 3,521 (5.0%): 24.4% in three provinces. Madrid registers 13.9 masts per municipality and Soria 1.8. The network follows the population, which explains why population coverage can be almost total while geographic coverage is not.

What is being withdrawn is fixed, not mobile

The obligation that falls away concerns fixed broadband and voice, not mobile. Mobile coverage is, in the order’s words, “equally a relevant element”, but the service that stops being guaranteed is the fixed one. For households, the file relies on a different kind of coverage: fibre at 95.72% of homes (30 June 2025), fixed wireless access at 99.15% and satellite at 100%. Satellite connections rose from 10,199 at the end of 2022 to 98,391 at the end of 2025, 0.5% of fixed broadband. The backstop for the last households is not 5G: it is satellite.

Limits

The registry is administrative and counts installations, not coverage; nPerf is a sample of Android users; the ministry’s data is reported by operators; and 100% satellite coverage is theoretical availability, with no price or quality. One doubt remains: whether the order’s 99.27% of territory is a transcription of the population figure or a measurement nobody has published. Until a source settles it, the highest share public data can support is the population one.

From 2027 there will be no designated operator and the social tariff is frozen. The argument is not whether there is coverage, but what “universal” means when it is counted in people.
