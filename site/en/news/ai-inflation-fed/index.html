AI enters the inflation debate: 129 countries above 2%

Two Federal Reserve officials now point to the data-centre build-out as demand pressure. The inflation map puts 129 of 187 countries above 2% in their latest reading, and the market is already pricing a rate rise on 9 December.

Newsroom · 25 November 2026 · 5 min read

At 13:30 London time today (08:30 in Washington) the October PCE is published, the price index the Federal Reserve uses as its compass. It lands with rates at 3.75%-4%, the market pricing a 25-basis-point rise on 9 December and the Nasdaq posting its worst week since March 2020.

On Monday Nvidia rose more than 30% after Jensen Huang's return and the S&P 500 gained 4.7%. On Tuesday the Nasdaq gave up 1.48%. On Wednesday the selling moved to Asia: SoftBank is down 13.8% in Tokyo, Tokyo Electron 11.8%, TSMC 6.7% in Taipei, and the Korea Exchange has triggered a sell-side sidecar with the Kospi opening 3.94% lower.

What the Fed says

On 19 November Fed Vice Chair Philip Jefferson described the rapid adoption of AI as a shock that hits supply and demand on different timelines: “Currently, the AI buildout is exerting upward pressure on demand and inflation”. In future, he added, productivity could push prices down.

Austan Goolsbee, president of the Chicago Fed, reached for “a pig through the python”: “almost unprecedented levels of business investment” that can overwhelm the economy's capacity, with inflation “borderline, disturbingly high”. Minutes of the 27-28 October meeting show almost all members judged further increases appropriate, and JPMorgan expects rises in December, January and March, to 4.50%-4.75%.

Method: 187 countries and a median

The map carries 205 countries and territories with annual World Bank data (FP.CPI.TOTL.ZG, updated on 8 October 2026); 187 have a figure. Sorted, the median is 3.20%. Above 2% sit 129 countries; above 5%, 47; above 10%, 23. Fourteen are in deflation.

Two caveats. First, 2% is the Fed's target, not a universal standard. Second, the data are annual and refer to the latest available year: 167 countries have a 2025 figure, seven have 2024 and three have 2023 or earlier. The United States shows 2.95% for 2024 because the World Bank has not yet published 2025, so that number does not capture the current upturn.

Three readings of the map

One: high inflation is not American. 69% of the countries with data closed their latest year above 2%, and one in four above 5%. Washington's rate policy explains part of the global price of money, not the whole map.

Two: the tail is still there. Turkey records 34.88% (2025), Nigeria 23.01% and Argentina 219.88% (2024). Venezuela appears at 254.95%, but the figure is from 2016: the map keeps the last published year. At the other end, France closed 2025 at 0.94% and China at 0.06%.

Three: the shared input is electricity. Australia reported 4.9% annual inflation this morning, with electricity up 25.2%: the same input data centres consume. The map does not show that AI caused that jump; it only marks where price pressure is already macroeconomic.

The channel is valuation, not earnings

The link between AI and rates runs through valuation, not company accounts. Goldman Sachs reckons the sell-off has cut the S&P 500's forward multiples by 25%, to 16 times earnings; UBS sums up the shift in the question: the debate is no longer whether investment will continue, but whether it can deliver an adequate return. With the 10-year Treasury around 4.9%, every point of policy rates discounts 2030 earnings that much harder.

That is why the return of a chief executive moved more than a trillion dollars in a day and unwound the next. AI is also a rates thesis: the more expensive funding gets, the more it costs to wait.

What comes next

The PCE today. The November jobs report on 4 December. The meeting the market has already priced on 8-9 December; on 26-27 January, the one that decides whether the Fed hikes three times or twice. If core PCE lands at the 3.2%-3.3% forecast, December is done and the argument moves to 2027.

Informative analysis, not investment advice. The map data are annual and reflect the latest available year for each country; they do not replace the monthly series published by national statistics offices.
