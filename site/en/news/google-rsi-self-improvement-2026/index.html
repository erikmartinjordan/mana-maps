Google is already running RSI loops: the AI that improves itself is not accelerating yet

AI writes 75% of Google's new code and DeepMind admits it runs self-improvement loops. Its own safety report finds no significant acceleration: 88% of code changes still need human steering. What Google, OpenAI, Anthropic and Microsoft are doing, and what it means.

Newsroom · 22 November 2026 · 9 min read

The question is no longer whether the big technology companies use artificial intelligence to build better artificial intelligence. They do. The question is how much, in which part of the process and with what human control. The distinction matters because “RSI” has become a label that can mean almost anything: from an agent rewriting a configuration file to a system that designs, trains and evaluates its successor without anyone at the wheel.

Google is the most instructive case. In April 2026, Sundar Pichai said 75% of all new code at the company was generated by AI and approved by engineers. In October, the Gemini 4 Argon safety report added the other half of the story: DeepMind reviewed 1,389 trajectories from its internal agent and found no significant end-to-end acceleration in its R&D. Code is being written faster; intelligence, for now, is not improving itself at the pace some had feared.

What RSI is — and what it is not

The idea is sixty years old. In 1965, I. J. Good described an “intelligence explosion”: a machine capable of designing machines better than itself, which would in turn design even better ones. The modern concept is more modest and more precise. RSI is a loop in which an AI system materially contributes to improving the system that produces it: it proposes changes, runs them, measures the result and keeps what works. If the loop closes, each generation starts from a better base and progress can stop being linear.

But not everything called RSI is RSI. A model that autocompletes code is not RSI. An agent that corrects its own mistakes within a session is not either: when the session ends, nothing persists. RSI requires the improvement to persist — in code, data, weights or the organisation — and to change how the next generation is produced. DeepMind frames it as three mechanisms: improving code and designs (architectures, algorithms, circuits), improving data (using deployment compute to generate training material) and specialising teams of agents that collaborate.

The scale the labs use. Epoch AI defines six automation levels, from AL0 (no AI) to AL5 (fully autonomous AI, with no humans in the loop). At AL3 the AI “collaborates” under close direction; at AL4 it “leads”: it completes most of a task from a high-level goal, with a human supervising. No lab has yet reported sustained AL5.

That gradation is the key to reading the headlines. Saying a company “uses RSI” may mean it has automated parts of the process, not that it has built a machine that reproduces itself. Most of what happened in 2026 belongs to the first category. What is remarkable is how quickly those parts have become important.

Google: yes, but in pieces

The most quoted figure is code. In October 2024, Pichai spoke of “more than a quarter” of new code being generated by AI. By autumn 2025, the figure had risen to 50%. In April 2026, to 75%. The trajectory is spectacular, though it measures one specific thing: new lines that a model proposes and an engineer reviews and accepts. It does not measure code quality, legacy code or net productivity.

The second figure is less well known. In its Gemini 4 Argon report, DeepMind says the share of fully delegated pull requests — changes submitted by an agent with no human writing the code — rose from 7.5% to 11.9% between April and September 2026. The other 88% still required multi-turn human steering or human authorship. In other words: 75% of new code may be AI-generated, but only about a tenth of the change work is handed over completely.

The part that does look like a closed loop is AlphaEvolve, Google DeepMind's agent that proposes code, scores it with an automatic evaluator and keeps the versions that improve the metric. In production it has recovered an average of 0.7% of Google's worldwide compute by optimising its data-centre scheduler; it sped up a Gemini training kernel by 23%, cutting training time by 1%; and it has helped design circuits for the next generation of TPUs. These are real, measured, deployed gains. They are also local: an automatic evaluator decides what “better” means, and the loop only works where success can be scored.

Pichai has been explicit about the limit. “We are pretty far from the end state of RSI,” he said in October. “The right way to describe it is, you have RSI loops along with AI researchers in various aspects of model development.” Co-founder Sergey Brin went further: Google is “starting to get onto that RSI treadmill.” The metaphor is apt: a treadmill that is moving, but with people walking alongside it.

The brake is not writing. It is reviewing

The central finding of DeepMind's report is not that AI is useless for research, but that the bottleneck has moved. If an agent can produce in minutes a change that used to take hours, the scarce work becomes deciding whether the change is correct, whether it generalises and whether it belongs in production. DeepMind measured this across 1,389 Argon trajectories: the longer the task, the less likely the model was to finish it alone and the more often a human stepped in. Automation is highest in “well-specified, verifiable execution tasks” and lowest in open-ended scientific ideation, novel experimental design and diagnosing subtle training instabilities.

The practical consequence is awkward for both easy narratives. Against the optimists, AI is not replacing the researcher: it is turning them into the director of a team of agents. Against the pessimists, the volume of work it absorbs is enormous and growing fast. The engineer who used to write code now reviews it, corrects course and decides which experiments are worth running. If review cannot scale at the same pace as generation, the whole system jams.

Anthropic: from 1% to 26% in six months

Anthropic has published the most aggressive series. Its internal R&D automation index puts Claude “leading” 26% of its model research and development tasks in August 2026, up from under 1% in February. More than 90% of measured work sits at AL3 or above — AI collaborates — and 0% at AL5: no task is considered fully autonomous. The company also says more than 80% of the code it merges into production was written by Claude, and that the typical engineer merges eight times as much code per day as in 2024, though it concedes that the metric overstates real productivity; an internal survey puts the true multiplier at around four.

The methodology deserves as much attention as the number. Anthropic sampled 20% of staff in the departments involved in the R&D loop every week, extracted some 15,000 tasks from Slack and internal documents, organised them into a 542-node tree and scored them with a Claude model. In human validation, the model's judgement matched people exactly 59% of the time; within one level, 97%. The company itself concedes that a judge from the same model family can repeat its errors and that independent verification is needed.

There are other signals. METR, the external group that measures agent capability, reviewed Anthropic's internal data and estimated that its capability scores were growing at 1.9 times the earlier rate since October 2025, with 80% of that acceleration attributable to AI. Anthropic said in its amended prospectus on 2 November that it “may imminently meet” its internal threshold for a doubling of acceleration, but not that it had observed it. That distinction matters: a projection is not a measurement.

OpenAI, Microsoft and the rest of the race

OpenAI was the first lab to say out loud that a model had helped build itself. In February 2026 it described GPT-5.3-Codex as “our first model that was instrumental in creating itself”: early versions were used to debug its training, manage its deployment and diagnose evaluation failures. In September, the company said it had reached its “automated research intern”, a system that completes well-defined tasks under human direction, and set March 2028 as the target for a full AI researcher. By November it was measuring 5.7 agent-workdays per researcher-workday, up from 0.5 at the start of the year.

The usage figures are as telling as the capability ones. By mid-August, the median OpenAI researcher was spending more than $600 a day on inference at API prices; the top 10% were above $7,000. But more than half of successful four-to-eight-hour tasks required at least one human intervention. The company says it plainly: it does not yet know how to reach aligned, full RSI safely.

Microsoft represents the other route: not closing the loop in a product, but publishing the machinery. Its Self-HPO framework alternates optimisation of the harness — prompts, tools, memory and control flow — with training the weights. Across three coding benchmarks, a 35-billion-parameter model rose from an average resolve rate of 57.8% to 64.8%. That is a research result, not a production metric, but it shows that self-improvement is no longer only a lab experiment: it is a technique others can replicate.

Meta and xAI have not published an index comparable to Anthropic's or DeepMind's. Their public evidence is papers and demos — agents that reward themselves, systems that edit their own harness — not an account of how much of their R&D is executed by AI. That absence is also a data point: in the RSI race, transparency is uneven and each company defines the metric that suits it.

The independent yardstick: from 3 minutes to more than 5 hours

Company metrics have a built-in problem: each measures what suits it. METR offers a different yardstick. Its task suite estimates how long an expert human would take to solve each problem and calculates the task duration an agent completes with 50% success. It does not measure how long a model works, but how far it gets unaided. Across the selected models, the jump is two orders of magnitude in under three years: from GPT-4's 3.5 minutes in March 2023 to Claude Opus 4.5's 5.3 hours in November 2025.

More releases, not necessarily faster

Another way to measure whether the machinery is accelerating is to count what labs publish. The AI Engineering keeps a record of 1,250 notable model releases since January 2023. Between January and October 2025, the six big labs published 77 models; in the same period of 2026, 121, 57% more. The aggregate hides the split: Google and OpenAI are flat (33→37 and 20→27), while Microsoft (9→21), Anthropic (5→11), Meta (5→10) and xAI (5→15) are rising. The point is not that AI is improving itself; it is that release cadence has become a strategy: more variants, more modalities and more intermediate versions. Publishing more is not the same as researching better.

The consequences: five fronts

1. The bottleneck moves to verification. If AI generates more code and more experiments than a human team can review, the limit stops being production and becomes trust. DeepMind sees it in pull requests; OpenAI, in long tasks; Anthropic, in code review. The consequence is organisational: fewer people writing, more people deciding, auditing and designing evaluators. And a new question: who verifies the verifier when the evaluator is also a model?

2. More capex, more concentration. Alphabet plans to invest $175-185 billion in 2026, almost six times the $31 billion of 2022. Bloomberg estimated in April that combined spending by the big technology companies would exceed $700 billion this year. If RSI compresses development cycles, the advantage compounds for whoever controls chips, power, data and distribution. Labs that depend on someone else's cloud do not compete on equal terms. The risk is not only economic: it is the concentration of power over a technology that increasingly designs part of itself.

3. Security changes shape. An agent that does research has access to sensitive code, data and models. RAND warns that compromising an internal agent could subtly influence the development of future systems. DeepMind concedes it cannot rule out Gemini 4 Argon crossing cyber or biosecurity thresholds in an unrestricted version. And the report uses an internal benchmark with a 90% pass requirement to rule out full automation; an independent LessWrong analysis noted that about a quarter of the scored tasks contained bugs that made them impossible to pass. The conclusion “no acceleration” is plausible, but it rests on an imperfect instrument.

4. Research work splits in two. The executable part — writing, testing, measuring, iterating — is automated quickly. The judgement part — choosing the problem, deciding which result to trust, knowing when to abandon a line — remains human and is what makes the difference. That does not mean there is no displacement: it means displacement is uneven. Junior profiles who learned by doing executable tasks have fewer rungs to climb. Companies that only measure lines of code or agent-workdays are measuring the easy part.

5. Governance arrives late. There is no common standard for reporting how much of a lab's R&D is executed by AI, how much compute goes to model-directed experiments or how much improvement is attributable to AI-generated ideas. RAND calls for a taxonomy and systematic reporting; Anthropic proposes an index and asks others to publish it; OpenAI says the metrics should be mandatory. Until verification exists, every figure is a statement by an interested party. And interested parties, in a race, tend to optimise their numbers.

What to watch in 2027

The first signal is AL5. It is currently zero in every published index. If a company starts reporting complete tasks with no humans in the loop, the debate changes in kind. The second is end-to-end acceleration: not the share of code, but the slope of capability per month. DeepMind published 0.091 points on its index before 1 April and 0.118 after; the difference was not statistically significant. If that slope takes off, the RSI discussion stops being speculative.

The third is review. The 11.9% of fully delegated pull requests at DeepMind is a small base; if it rises to 25% or 40% without quality collapsing, the bottleneck will truly have moved. The fourth is external verification: a third party able to replicate the benchmark, audit the internal data and check that the judge is not rewarding its own model. The fifth is the calendar: OpenAI is aiming for an automated researcher in March 2028; METR calculates that, if the trend holds, a 200-hour task horizon would arrive in July 2027. These are extrapolations, not promises.

The provisional conclusion is less cinematic than the headlines and more interesting. Google is using RSI, but in fragments: code, kernels, data-centre scheduling, circuits, evaluation. It has not closed the full loop, nor has it found the acceleration that would justify the fear. What it has found is something more prosaic and harder to manage: a growing ability to produce changes faster than humans can understand them. The problem of 2027 will not be that AI improves itself. It will be that improvement comes so fast that nobody has time to check it.
