Spain adds airline seats as Europe cuts capacity for 2027

Airlines are preparing summer 2027 with fewer seats and higher fares. Spain is going the other way: it will be Europe's biggest air market this winter, and the map of international arrivals helps explain why.

Newsroom · 23 November 2026 · 5 min read

Jet fuel is trading at $169 a barrel, 88.6% higher than a year ago, after peaking at almost $200 in October. Faced with that bill, carriers are already moving ahead of the peak season: El País reported on Monday that airlines expect higher fares and fewer seats for next summer. The International Air Transport Association (IATA) expects the industry's net profit to halve this year, from $45bn to $23bn, on a 2% margin, the thinnest since the post-Covid recovery.

The winter is already booked

The adjustment does not start in summer. For the winter season, from November to March, European airlines have scheduled 501 million seats, 1.6% more than a year earlier, according to the consultancy OAG. That headline number hides two opposite directions. Spain will sell close to 140 million seats, 6.4% more, overtaking the United Kingdom (134 million, 1% lower) as the continent's largest market, according to Cirium. On those two figures the sum is simple: almost one in four seats sold in Europe this winter — 27.9% — is on a Spanish airport.

Within Spain there are nuances too. Iberia will grow by close to 5%, Vueling will fall back 1% and Italy will add 11% as Wizz Air opens bases in Valencia, Madrid and Santiago.

The other side of the same sum

Across the rest of the continent the arithmetic runs the other way. Germany and France are cutting by around 1%. Lufthansa faces a fuel bill of €9.3bn, €2bn more than a year earlier, and has already removed the equivalent of five aircraft from its main brand. IAG expects to spend €8.9bn and has adjusted its fourth-quarter network: 1% less at British Airways, 6% at Aer Lingus, 8% at Vueling and 28% at Level. Ryanair has dropped two million winter seats and easyJet up to 1.4 million. Traffic is already falling in Frankfurt (−10%), Munich (−8%), Istanbul (−7%) and Heathrow (−5%).

The hit is now measured across the whole industry: IBA Insight puts it at about $12bn for European carriers. The weakest have given way: Volotea and Plus Ultra are renegotiating their debt, AlbaStar has ceased operations and Air Baltic has cut its fleet from 54 aircraft to 36.

The map: who receives the tourists

The map ranks countries by international arrivals, using UN Tourism data for 2024. France leads with 102 million; Spain, on 93.76 million, sits 8.24 million behind, or 92% of the French figure. The United States receives 72.4 million, Turkey 60.6 and Italy 57.7. The United Kingdom, which until this winter was Europe's largest air market, receives only 38.2 million: much of its seat capacity exists to fly Britons out, not to bring tourists in.

That is the clue: capacity follows the demand able to pay higher fares, and Spain is the world's second destination and Europe's first by arrivals. The map does not measure prices or load factors, and it does not on its own explain airline decisions; what it does is put a scale on the business at stake.

What comes next

The peak season is the open question. Ryanair went as far as predicting rises of up to 20% on short-haul fares for 2027, in the words of its chief executive, Michael O'Leary, and Lufthansa expects double-digit increases. The fuel hedges that shielded European carriers — they covered around 70% of consumption before the crisis — are expiring, and every renewal carries the new price.

In December, IATA will revise its forecasts for 2027. Before that, the industry will watch two things: whether jet fuel stays below $170 and whether demand holds without a sharp fare correction. The asymmetry, for now, is the only certainty: the same fuel that forces cuts in Munich is funding expansion at Barajas.
