Spain's minimum wage rises to €1,286: where it sits in Europe

The cabinet approved a 5.3% rise to €1,286 gross over 14 payments on Tuesday. The series of statutory minimums measures the distance that still separates Spain from Portugal, and from France.

Newsroom · 18 November 2026 · 5 min read

Spain's statutory minimum wage will be €1,286 gross a month over 14 payments in 2027. The cabinet approved the increase on Tuesday 17 November: €65 more per payslip, 5.3% on top of the current €1,221 and €18,004 a year, the equivalent of €42.87 a day.

The figure clears two benchmarks. Consumer prices had risen 4.3% in the year to October, and the labour ministry's expert commission had recommended 4.9%, to €1,281. The government estimates the measure reaches about 2.7 million employees, close to 9% of the workforce, with a heavier incidence among young people, women and temporary contracts.

What the government approved

The minimum wage is set by decree, and the rise comes with two tax changes and one for public employees. The finance ministry lifts the income-tax withholding threshold to €18,004, so anyone on the minimum wage will see no deduction on their payslip — since 2024 the exemption worked through a rebate at filing — and extends deductions to incomes of up to €23,172. Civil servants get 4.5% more and legal-aid lawyers an 8% increase in their compensation scales.

The deal is not unanimous. CCOO and UGT welcomed it but had asked for 7% and for changes to stop employers absorbing the rise through supplements. The CEOE employers' federation, which did not back it, complained that the ministry had informed rather than consulted it; its president, Antonio Garamendi, placed the decision in the campaign for the 29 November general election.

Three minimums, three distances

The map brings together the statutory minimum wage of 205 countries from the ILO (ILOSTAT) and WageIndicator, converted into dollars at World Bank exchange rates in its December 2024 edition. The figures are nominal, gross and monthlyised over 12 payments: they leave out living costs, supplements and each country's tax treatment, and countries without a statutory minimum — Sweden, Denmark, Finland, Iceland, Norway, Austria, Italy and Switzerland — appear in grey.

In that series Portugal's minimum was worth €993 a month (€11,920 a year) and Greece's €1,058 (€12,694). Spain's €18,004 for 2027 sits 51.0% above Portugal and 41.8% above Greece, and also clears Slovenia's €15,781 (+14.1%).

North of the Pyrenees the gap reverses. France's minimum stood at €1,802 a month and €21,622 a year, leaving Spain 16.7% below. Germany recorded €2,033 and €24,394 (−26.2%), the Netherlands €2,786 and €33,437 (−46.2%) and Ireland, the highest in the series, €2,911 and €34,928 (−48.5%).

What the figure does not measure

Comparing statutory minimums is not the same as comparing living standards. The map is nominal: €1,000 does not buy the same in Lisbon as in Munich, and the indicator ignores supplements, collective agreements and hours actually worked. Nor is it a snapshot of a single year: Spain appears with its 2024 figure, not its 2027 one, and neighbouring countries will negotiate their own increases. The payment structure adds another difference, because Spain pays its minimum over 14 instalments while the map monthlyises over 12; that is why the jump from €16,130 to €18,004 (+11.6%) spans three years and does not match the 5.3% of a single year.

What comes next

The 2027 minimum applies from January's first payslip. The tax side needs a decree-law backed by the permanent congressional committee, and the 4.5% for civil servants must be ratified in the chamber. Unions have asked for the revision to be carried into collective agreements: the expert commission's report warned that around 40% of the agreements it analysed set base pay below the statutory minimum. With an increase of nearly 75% in eight years, the argument is no longer whether the floor rises, but how much of the pay scale it drags with it.
