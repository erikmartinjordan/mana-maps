Midterms with record women candidates and a 28.9% female Congress

The United States votes on Tuesday 3 November for the whole House, a third of the Senate and 36 governorships. There have never been so many women on the ballot: 590 filed to run for the House and 26 are nominees for governor. The women-in-parliament map puts the starting point at 28.9% of seats.

The United States holds its midterm elections on Tuesday 3 November. All 435 House seats are up, along with a third of the Senate and 36 governorships. It is also the most expensive cycle on record: more than $11bn in political advertising, according to the firm AdImpact, cited by Time.

What has happened

The cycle arrives with a record number of women candidates. According to the Center for American Women and Politics (CAWP) at Rutgers University, 590 women filed to run for the House of Representatives, above the previous high of 583, set in 2020 and matched in 2022. A record 3,824 women are standing for state legislatures in 46 states, up from 3,621 in 2022, and they make up 35.5% of all candidates.

The governor races carry the other historic figure: 26 women are nominees to lead a state, the highest number CAWP has recorded. Several could break glass ceilings on Tuesday. Peggy Flanagan, in Minnesota, and Mary Peltola, in Alaska, could become the first Native American women in the Senate. Deb Haaland, in New Mexico, is bidding to become one of the first Native American women to lead a state.

Keisha Lance Bottoms, in Georgia, and Lisa Demuth, in Minnesota, could become the country's first Black women governors. Chris Pappas, in New Hampshire, could become the first openly gay man elected to the Senate, and Hakeem Jeffries the first Black Speaker of the House if the Democrats win a majority. The record is not shared evenly between the parties: women make up 42% of congressional Democrats and 15% of congressional Republicans, according to CAWP data reported by Gray DC.

The map: where the country starts from

The map colours 205 countries and territories by the share of parliamentary seats held by women. The data come from the World Bank (indicator SG.GEN.PARL.ZS) and refer to 2025, the last completed year. The darker the country, the higher the share of women in its chamber.

The United States sits at 28.9%. Mexico is above half the seats (50.2%); Spain records 44.3%, the United Kingdom 40.5% and France 36.2%. Germany (32.4%) and Canada (30.3%) are closer to the US figure. At the other end, Brazil (18.1%), Japan (15.7%) and India (13.8%) are further behind.

The comparison has limits, and the map itself makes them visible. It measures seats held, not candidacies: a country can field many women and elect few, or the reverse. Nor does it predict Tuesday's result. Electoral systems, quotas and parity rules change completely how votes turn into seats, and the figure predates the chamber being elected now. What it does provide is a scale: even with a record field, the United States would need to add 21.3 points to match Mexico and 15.4 to reach Spain.

What comes next

Results will emerge overnight on 3 to 4 November, Central European Time. The open question is whether a record field turns into a record number of seats. CAWP tracks a symbolic threshold: Democratic women reaching 100 House seats, a mark the party has never hit. Running is not the same as winning, and the centre notes that the gain depends on the most competitive districts.

The 120th Congress is sworn in in January. If the result leaves the United States below 30%, the country will remain well behind its European peers and Mexico, and the map will not reflect it until the next annual update, because the available figure is for 2025. The governor races, with 26 women nominees, offer the fastest route to visible change: several of the tightest contests are decided on Tuesday.

The question the map leaves is not how many women run, but how many govern. That answer will arrive with the count.
