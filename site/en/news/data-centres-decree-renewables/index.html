Spain's data centre decree opens a legal and European front

Industry body SpainDC is finalising a Supreme Court challenge and Madrid's regional government says it will take the rule to Brussels. The decree requires 80% of a data centre's electricity to be renewable and generated in the same hour.

Newsroom · 24 November 2026 · 6 min read

Royal Decree 1032/2026 was published in Spain's official gazette on 21 November and came into force the following day. Within a week it has opened three fronts: industry body SpainDC is finalising an appeal to the Supreme Court with a request to suspend the rule while it is heard, Madrid's regional government says it will seek interim measures and denounce the decree in Europe, and more than 160 organisations have signed a manifesto against the text.

The conflict stems from an electricity obligation with few equivalents in Europe. Every data centre must show that 80% of its consumption comes from renewable sources and that this energy was generated in the same hour it was used. The rule covers facilities of 10 MW or more and adds an additionality test: the plants backing that consumption must have entered service no more than 36 months before the centre starts running.

What the text requires

The decree has two halves. The energy half sets efficiency standards, requires new centres to reach the highest category on the European water-use scale and introduces compensation for anyone short of the 80%: €6 to €25 per MWh depending on the shortfall in additional generation, and €2 to €16 per MWh depending on the hourly deviation. The digital half adds resilience and sovereignty requirements.

There is an escape hatch: once renewables exceed 90% of the national mix, both tests are deemed met. And centres that join the automatic power-reduction scheme, which disconnects them first, may justify 60% during peak hours until the end of 2028 and 70% afterwards.

The legal front

SpainDC, which represents 350 companies, argues the rule cannot be met in practice. Its chief executive, Begoña Villacís, put the sector's consumption at 0.8% of Spain's electricity and said it already runs on 98% renewables. In the same public statement she noted that 1,179 GWh of renewable energy were curtailed in June and 1,449 GWh in July, a record, for want of demand to absorb them. The association cites €66,900m of investment committed or planned.

At its annual congress in Madrid on Monday the association asked companies and trade bodies to join the legal fight. It also presented a Wood Mackenzie report estimating that 3 GW of demand in 2030 would raise the final retail electricity price by about €1.5 per MWh, or 1.3%. The ecological transition ministry argues the opposite: without the rule the impact on bills would reach €5bn and industrial consumers would pay up to 25% more, according to figures deputy prime minister Sara Aagesen gave after the cabinet meeting.

The map: the scale of what is being asked

The map collects the world's 18 largest solar parks, with 96,619 MW between them. Twelve are Chinese and account for 82,664 MW, or 85.6%. The largest, Talatan, has 21,000 MW; the second, Ningdong, 18,320. None is in Spain or anywhere else in the EU: the list is split between China, India, the UAE, Egypt and Turkey.

That scale helps size the requirement. An EY analysis of the draft calculated that serving 3.5 GW of data centre demand with 80% hourly matching and additionality would need 49.8 GW of new generation and storage, of which 26.8 GW would be solar. That is 1.28 times the capacity of the largest solar park on the planet. The final text softened some requirements, but keeps the hourly test the industry sees as the crux.

What happens next

The appeal to the Supreme Court's administrative chamber could be filed this week, according to the specialist press. The European Commission is still working on the Cloud and AI Development Act, the law Brussels wants to use to coordinate data centre rollout across the union.

The calendar adds another variable: the general election on 29 November leaves open the survival of a decree approved six days before polling day. Until then the industry has two routes running at once, the legal and the political, and one question on the table: how much additional renewable capacity it is willing to build to keep AI growing in Spain.
