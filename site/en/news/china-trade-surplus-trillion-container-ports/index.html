China's surplus tops $1 trillion as Xi and Trump meet in Shenzhen

Xi Jinping and Donald Trump meet in Shenzhen on Thursday with tariffs and chips on the table. Behind them sits a goods surplus of $1.04 trillion in ten months. The map of the world's 50 busiest container ports shows where that cargo leaves from.

Newsroom · 19 November 2026 · 5 min read

Xi Jinping and Donald Trump began talks in Shenzhen on Thursday on the sidelines of the APEC summit, seeking to steady the relationship between the world's two largest economies with tariffs, rare earths and advanced chips on the table, according to CNA. It is their third face-to-face meeting this year, after Beijing in May and Washington in September. A three-way meeting with Vladimir Putin was scheduled for later in the day, focused on nuclear arms and the wars in Ukraine and Iran.

The backdrop is a trade balance that keeps growing. China's customs administration reported a surplus of $1.04 trillion between January and October on 10 November, the second consecutive year above the trillion mark and a pace that puts 2025's record of $1.2 trillion within reach, according to the Associated Press. In October exports rose 35.2% year on year and imports 41.6%. The monthly surplus narrowed to $105.3bn from $133.5bn in September as imports, crude oil in particular, gathered pace.

A number measured in containers

The map plots the world's 50 busiest container ports at their real locations, with 2024 traffic in TEUs (twenty-foot equivalent units). Together they moved 601.5 million TEUs, according to Lloyd's List and the World Shipping Council.

Fifteen of those 50 ports are in mainland China and handled 268.8 million TEUs, 44.7% of the total. Six of the top ten are Chinese. Shanghai, at 51.5 million, is the only port above 50 million in a year and accounts on its own for 8.6% of the list's traffic. It is followed by Ningbo-Zhoushan (39.3 million), Shenzhen (33.4), Qingdao (30.9) and Guangzhou (26.1).

Outside China the scale changes. Singapore ranks second with 41.1 million TEUs. The five US ports on the list add up to 38.3 million (6.4%) and the seven EU ports to 54.6 million (9.1%), led by Rotterdam (13.8) and Antwerp (13.5). Spain appears twice: Valencia with 5.5 million in 38th place and Algeciras with 4.7 million in 44th.

One limit is worth keeping in mind: the map counts containers, not value. Most of the semiconductors at the centre of the dispute travel by air, and cars on dedicated ships, so the map measures the volume of China's exports rather than their technological content.

Hong Kong: the financial gateway and the physical one

Hong Kong ranks 12th with 13.7 million TEUs. Across the border, Shenzhen handled 33.4 million, 2.4 times as much. Add the Hong Kong port to the mainland ones and the top 50 becomes 47.0% Chinese. The city that was for decades the gateway for Chinese goods now carries less than its neighbours in the Pearl River Delta.

The other gateway is narrowing too. Hong Kong's Hang Seng was down 2.1% on Thursday at about 22,800 points, its lowest level since late June and a third straight session of losses. Technology stocks dragged the index after Tencent reported third-quarter revenue up 8% — two points slower than in the previous quarter — and adjusted operating profit down 6%, while capital spending on AI data centres surged, according to market data compiled by Trading Economics. New US restrictions on AI chip exports and remote cloud access are adding to the pressure.

The map does not explain that fall: it measures cargo, not multiples. What it does show is the imbalance Trump and Xi are negotiating. China sells far more to the world than it buys, and the infrastructure that makes it possible is concentrated on a single coast.

What comes next

The US-China trade truce runs out on 10 January 2027, and both sides arrive in Shenzhen with unfinished business on chips and rare earths. For Chinese technology companies, the AI bill shows up in margins before it shows up in revenue: more capital spending, less operating profit. For the ports, the number that matters is still volume. If the surplus beats 2025's $1.2 trillion record this year, it will leave through the same cranes the map shows.
