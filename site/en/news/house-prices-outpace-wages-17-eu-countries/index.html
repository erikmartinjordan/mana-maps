House prices outpace wages in 17 EU countries

Eurostat updated its house price-to-income index on 3 November: it deteriorated in 17 of the 27 member states between 2015 and 2025. Spain's ratio is 24.5% worse, and the provincial map shows that in 2026 the rise spares neither expensive provinces nor cheap ones.

Newsroom · 16 November 2026 · 5 min read

Housing has grown more expensive than incomes in 17 of the European Union's 27 countries over the past decade. That is the reading of Eurostat's standardised house price-to-income index, updated on 3 November, in which Spain shows the sharpest deterioration of any large economy: 24.5% between 2015 and 2025. Only Portugal (60.8%), the Netherlands (34.3%), Hungary (26.5%) and Czechia (25.8%) fare worse across the 27.

An index that measures a gap, not prices

Eurostat's figure does not say how much a home costs; it tracks how its price has moved relative to disposable income per person. With 2025 as the base of 100, Spain's index stood at 80.3 in 2015: housing has become 24.5% more expensive than incomes in ten years. Portugal started from 62.2, the lowest in the table, so its 60.8% also reflects a depressed starting point.

The other three big euro-area economies move the other way. Germany worsens by 1.6%, France improves by 5.6% and Italy by 11.9%. The average across the 27 is 6.8%. At the opposite end from Portugal, Romania (−42.8%), Finland (−25.2%) and Bulgaria (−16.2%) have narrowed the distance between prices and incomes since 2015.

The map: the rise does not discriminate

Eurostat's index is national. To get closer to the ground, the map compiles the average appraised value of free-market housing across the 52 provinces and autonomous cities in the second quarter of 2026, with its annual change, from the housing ministry's appraised-value statistics. It is a valuation figure rather than a completed sale price, and its latest reading is the end of June.

No province fell in that quarter. The simple average rises 12.7% and 39 of the 52 are above 10%. Madrid commands the highest price (€4,089.6/m²), followed by the Balearic Islands (3,980.8) and Gipuzkoa (3,514.6); Ciudad Real has the lowest (854.2). The gap between top and bottom is 4.8 times.

The pace of the increase, though, does not follow that order. Our own calculation across the map's 52 provinces produces a counter-intuitive result: the ten most expensive rise 11.5% on average and the ten cheapest 11.0%, and the correlation between price level and annual change is close to zero (−0.01). Ciudad Real, the most affordable province in the country, is up 14.3%, ahead of Madrid (12.6%); Zamora (+14.0%) and Teruel (+14.2%) repeat the pattern near the bottom of the table. The steepest increases are in Segovia (+18.1%), Valencia (+17.6%) and Toledo (+17.3%); the smallest is Ourense (+5.6%).

What comes next

The ministry publishes the series quarterly, so the July-to-September reading, still pending, will show whether the pace is easing. On the regulatory side, the first of the housing decree-laws has cleared confirmation in the permanent committee of Congress, as Vozpópuli reported. And in Portugal, where the rental reform is still under debate, thousands marched on 14 November in around 30 cities — some 60,000 in Lisbon, according to the organisers — and an encampment has kept about 40 tents in Lisbon's Restauradores square since the weekend.

The map does not explain why prices are rising in provinces where demand is weaker, nor does it replace income data: it only shows that, between April and June 2026, the increase reached everywhere. With the gap between prices and incomes at its highest since 2015, the question is how long the rise can run.
