Barcelona: about 100 arrested as a youth protest turns violent

A gathering called on social media turned into looting, barricades and 33 fires in central Barcelona on Saturday. Police arrested about 100 people, a quarter of them minors. The unemployment map helps make sense of the labour-market backdrop to Europe's youth protests, but it does not explain what happened on its own.

Central Barcelona saw an afternoon of unrest on Saturday 24 October. Shortly after 18.00, an unauthorised gathering called on social media brought hundreds of young people to Plaça de Catalunya. At its peak, Catalonia's interior ministry estimated some 600 people. The gathering turned into looting, burning barricades and clashes with the Mossos d'Esquadra.

What happened

The incidents spread from Plaça de Catalunya to La Rambla, Portal de l'Àngel and nearby streets. Groups built barricades from bins, motorbikes and bicycles, set fire to street furniture and terrace awnings, and smashed the windows of electronics shops, jewellers and flower stalls. El Corte Inglés closed its doors with customers inside, according to El País.

The call circulated on social media under the slogan «Institut Indignats», a nod to France's «lycées indignés» movement. Catalonia's interior department sees a possible «echo» of protests in several European cities, some of which have turned violent, according to Heraldo. Interior minister Núria Parlon described those involved as unorganised groups who were not defending any right; Mayor Jaume Collboni called it gratuitous violence and vandalism.

Catalan president Salvador Illa said on X that violence had no place in Catalonia, that protesting was a democratic right, and that attacking, intimidating or causing damage was not. Collboni said the vandalism «will not go unpunished», including legal consequences for the guardians of any minors involved, according to Reuters. The gathering took place next to a housing encampment that has occupied the square for three weeks, but its organisers blocked the demonstrators from joining. Its spokesperson, Kiara Monroy, said they did not want a protest built peacefully over weeks to be turned violent.

The same day, France saw another day of protests over the cost of living and education. The French interior ministry put the turnout at 410,000 and said nearly 1,000 people had been arrested, according to the BBC; unions put the figure at 800,000. Further marches are called for 31 October and 5 November, the latter with a proposed general strike, according to El País.

Eurostat puts EU unemployment at 6.1% in August 2026 and youth unemployment at 15.4%. In Spain, total unemployment was 10.0% and youth unemployment 22.7%. These are monthly figures, distinct from the annual ILO estimates for 2025 used in the map.

The map: unemployment in the background

The map covers 205 countries and colours each by its total unemployment rate where data are available, the share of the labour force looking for work without finding it. Among the countries cited, Spain has the highest value: 10.4% in 2025. France follows at 7.5%, then Italy (6.4%), Portugal (6.2%), Belgium (5.9%) and Germany (3.7%).

The comparison helps set the context, but it does not order the protests. France, with a lower rate than Spain, saw the largest turnout and nearly 1,000 arrests. Spain, with the highest rate in the group, saw serious unrest in Barcelona, with about 100 arrests. Italy and Portugal, with lower rates, have also seen demonstrations. The map does not establish a mechanical link between unemployment and the streets: it shows a labour market with deep differences.

How to read the map

Each country is coloured by its unemployment rate. Lighter shades mean a lower rate, darker shades a higher one. Click a country to open a card with the value, year and source. Zoom out and the flat map becomes a 3D globe you can rotate; the 2D/3D button returns you to the flat view.

The unit is the share of the labour force, not the absolute number of unemployed people. The data are modelled estimates from the International Labour Organization for 2025, distributed by the World Bank, which updated the series on 8 October 2026. Most countries have 2025 data; some carry earlier years and 24 have no data. It is a national average: it does not distinguish between regions or between town and countryside.

What the map does not say

The map measures total unemployment, not youth unemployment. It does not measure housing, the cost of living, education spending, public services, temporary work, wages or the informal economy. Nor does it say anything about the motives behind a gathering that, according to the authorities, had no concrete demands and was not linked to the housing encampment.

The map does not explain the outbreak. It shows a labour market with deep differences, but Saturday's gathering was a call circulated on social media without demands, and the authorities describe it as gratuitous violence. Blaming unemployment would be a hypothesis, not a fact.

What comes next

The investigation remains open. The mayor and the interior minister did not rule out further arrests and warned of consequences for the guardians of minors. The housing encampment continues in the square, with no link to the unrest, according to the city council.

In France, further marches are called for 31 October and 5 November. Eurostat will publish September unemployment data on 30 October. The map does not predict what happens next, but it is a reminder that youth discontent has a labour-market backdrop that one night of unrest cannot capture.
