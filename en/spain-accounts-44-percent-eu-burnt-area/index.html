Spain accounts for 44% of the EU's burnt area this year

Spain has burnt around 299,000 hectares so far in 2026, 44% of all the land scorched by fire across the European Union, according to a report published on Wednesday by Zero Carbon Analytics using EFFIS data. The map of the world's worst wildfires puts the figure alongside other episodes, although it does not measure the current season.

The report, released on 28 October and built on European Forest Fire Information System (EFFIS) data up to 23 September, puts the EU total at around 680,000 hectares. That is almost twice the 2006-2025 average for the same point in the year and the third-worst figure since the series began in 2006, behind 2022 and 2025.

What happened

Spain accounts for 44.0% of the EU's burnt area, at close to 299,000 hectares. That is 3.4 times its 2006-2025 average for the same date. Italy contributes 16.6% and France 14.4%, with 97,754 hectares: the largest figure in its own series and almost 50% above its previous record, set in 2022. The Mediterranean group of member states accounts for 94% of the EU total.

Most of the damage was done in July. That month the EU set a record of around 353,000 hectares, almost four times the 2006-2025 July average and 27% above the previous high, of 2022. Spain beat its own monthly record by 21%. Across the EU, a typical July day burnt 5,615 hectares against 914 in 2009-2025, and 78 fires of 500 hectares or more were recorded.

The fires were not confined to the south. Belgium, Germany, Hungary, Poland and Slovakia also burnt above their averages, though countries outside the Mediterranean group make up just 6.4% of the total.

The report links the meteorological backdrop to human-caused warming. It cites a World Weather Attribution analysis finding that conditions such as those seen in central Spain in July are now at least 20 times more likely than in a pre-industrial climate and can be expected roughly once every six years. In south-west France those conditions are expected about once every 20 years.

The fires also released 29.5 million tonnes of CO₂ by 23 September, 1.4 times the 2003-2025 average. On 26 July, PM2.5 pollution reached a daily mean of 96 micrograms per cubic metre in Villa del Prado, west of Madrid, roughly six times the WHO daily guideline of 15, and 115 in Bordeaux. The bill is still open: a preliminary AccuWeather estimate puts the cost of Europe's 2026 fires at €19.1 billion, and only about a quarter of the EU's climate-related losses between 1980 and 2024 were insured.

Joanne Bentley-McKune, the report's lead author, said Europe was witnessing a dangerous structural shift in its wildfire landscape. Treating wildfires only as emergencies to respond to was no longer viable, she argued, because human-caused warming is multiplying the days with extreme fire weather.

The map: where Spain's season sits among the megafires

The map gathers 20 of the most devastating fires in recent history, from Australia's Black Saturday in 2009 to the Canadian fires of 2023. Each point is an episode with its burnt area, death toll, year and location, drawn from the Global Fire Emissions Database (GFED4.1).

Two entries help to place the Spanish season. The 2025 Iberian fires are listed at around 100,000 hectares across Spain and Portugal; Spain's 2026 figure alone is almost three times that. The 2022 European season, the worst in the recent series, appears at around 785,000 hectares across Portugal, Spain and France: Spain has burnt 38% of that total this year.

The global scale gives a better sense of magnitude. The Canadian fires of 2023 burnt around 18.5 million hectares and the Australian fires of 2019-2020 around 18.6 million: some 62 times the area burnt in Spain this year. The map does not measure the current season or replace EFFIS, and the methodologies are not identical: it is there to compare orders of magnitude, not to settle an annual balance.

What comes next

EFFIS updates its figures every week. In the latest snapshot, from 22 October, the EU had reached 699,310 hectares for 2026. By the end of September 2025, the worst year in the series, the figure was already above 1,027,351 hectares. The final 2026 balance will not be known until the year closes.

The political season is not over either. The European Commission presented its proposed Climate Resilience Framework on 27 October, which must now be negotiated with the European Parliament and member states. The issue returns to the international agenda at COP31, held in Antalya, Türkiye, from 9 to 20 November; 17 November is dedicated to land use and forest ecosystems.

For Spain, the immediate question is how much of the 299,000 hectares recovers and who pays for restoration. The report stresses that land management can reduce local risk but cannot replace emissions cuts: at 3.6°C of warming, burnt area would still grow across 55% of Europe even if investment in prevention is maintained.
