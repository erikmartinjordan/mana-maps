Delhi air improves to 'moderate' but the forecast stays 'poor'

India's capital woke on Monday to an air quality index of 164, in the 'moderate' band, but five of its stations were still 'poor' and the official forecast expects pollution to worsen between 26 and 28 October. The emergency plan was activated on 21 October. The PM2.5 map helps set out the underlying problem.

Delhi's air improved on Monday, but the relief may not last. The air quality index (AQI) stood at 164 at 7am local time, according to the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB). That is the 'moderate' band (101-200), better than Saturday's 191, but five stations were still 'poor' and the official forecast expects the AQI to return to that range between 26 and 28 October.

What happened

The CPCB calculates the AQI from several pollutants; PM2.5 was the lead pollutant at 30 of the 55 reporting stations. Jahangirpuri recorded the highest reading, 246, followed by Vivek Vihar (235) and CRRI Mathura Road (231). The wider capital region reached 168 on Sunday and white foam reappeared on the Yamuna, according to The Times of India.

On 21 October, the Commission for Air Quality Management (CAQM) invoked Stage 1 of the Graded Response Action Plan (GRAP) after the daily average AQI reached 245, according to the Indian government. It bans coal and firewood in tandoor ovens, restricts diesel generators, requires dust controls at construction sites and allows visibly polluting vehicles to be impounded.

On Sunday, Delhi's environment minister, Manjinder Singh Sirsa, met police commanders to coordinate winter measures, according to Hindustan Times. The problem recurs every year: weaker winds and lower temperatures hamper dispersion, and stubble burning and fireworks add to it, The Hindu notes.

The map: chronic exposure, not a daily episode

The map colours 195 countries and territories by the annual average exposure of their populations to PM2.5, measured in micrograms per cubic metre (µg/m³). The data are for 2023 and come from the World Bank, drawing on the WHO Global Health Observatory.

India records 54.1 µg/m³, more than ten times the WHO's annual guideline of 5 µg/m³. It is not an exception: Bangladesh reaches 69.3, Pakistan 58.4 and Nepal 53.2; Finland falls to 4.6 and the highest value is Qatar's 107.8. The map places India among the world's most exposed regions, but it does not explain this episode: it measures an annual national average, whereas the AQI is a daily index shaped by traffic, construction, stubble burning and wind.

How to read the map

Each country is shaded from light to dark according to its exposure. Tap a country to open a card with the value, year and source. Zoom out and the flat map becomes a 3D globe; the 2D/3D button returns to the flat view.

The unit is µg/m³ and the figure is a population-weighted annual average, not a daily reading. It refers to 2023 and does not distinguish between a megacity and rural areas: the national average cannot say what the air is like in a particular Delhi street.

What the map does not say

It does not measure PM10, ozone, nitrogen dioxide or carbon monoxide, nor indoor pollution. It does not identify sources or distinguish between days with a temperature inversion and windy days. Nor does it measure access to healthcare.

The AQI is a composite scale, not a PM2.5 concentration: the same value can come from particles, ozone or nitrogen dioxide. The map and the Delhi figure are therefore not directly comparable.

The map shows that India is part of the world's most chronically exposed region to fine particles, far above the WHO guideline. It does not show why Delhi's air worsened this week or how long the episode will last: that requires the CPCB's daily data and the weather forecast.

What comes next

The early warning system expects a 'poor' AQI from 26 to 28 October and 'poor' or 'moderate' afterwards, according to its bulletin of 25 October. The mixing height will fall from 2,200 to 1,300 metres and the ventilation index from 11,000 to 3,800 m²/s; below 6,000 with winds under 10 km/h, dispersion is unfavourable.

Stage 1 of the GRAP applies when the AQI is 'poor'; the framework provides for further restrictions if it enters the 'very poor' band (301-400).
